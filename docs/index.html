What movie ratings and box office data tell us

By John Shetron · Financial Data Analytics · MovieLens + TMDB data

This report analyzes a 400,000-rating sample that 125,278 MovieLens users gave to 9,843 movies between 1996 and 2023, joined with box-office budget and revenue figures from The Movie Database (TMDB) for the subset of those films with financial data available. It looks at which genres get watched and rated the most, which ones are actually worth the money, and how Hollywood spending and audience taste have shifted across decades.

Finding 01 · Engagement

A handful of genres draw far more ratings than the rest

Grouping every movie by its primary genre and counting the ratings each group collected shows a steep drop-off after the top few categories. — alone accounts for a meaningful share of all ratings in the dataset — more evidence that a handful of mainstream genres carry most of the viewing activity, while niche genres like Documentary or War see a fraction of the engagement.

Finding 02 · Quality

The most-watched genres aren't the highest-rated ones

Ranking genres by their average rating instead of rating volume flips the leaderboard: smaller, less-frequently-rated genres tend to score higher, while some of the most popular genres land closer to the middle of the pack. This is a common pattern in ratings data — a genre with fewer, more deliberate viewers often rates higher than one with a huge, casual audience.

Average rating, by primary genre (min. 200 ratings)
Finding 03 · Activity over time

Rating activity is uneven year to year, not steadily growing

The number of ratings logged per year swings considerably across the dataset's 1996–2023 span, with visible spikes rather than a smooth upward trend. These swings line up with MovieLens's own data-collection history more than with any real change in movie-watching habits, which is an important caveat for anyone using this data to talk about audience trends.

Ratings logged per year
Finding 04 · Rating trends

Average ratings have drifted over the years

Looking at the mean rating given per year (rather than the count) shows a gentler but still noticeable drift. A small change like this, spread across tens of thousands of ratings, is easy to miss in a single summary statistic but shows up clearly once it's plotted year by year.

Finding 05 · Box office

A small set of genres account for most tracked box-office revenue

Restricting to the — movies with usable TMDB budget and revenue figures and summing revenue by primary genre shows box-office money concentrated even more sharply than rating volume was. — leads the pack, reflecting how blockbuster-scale spending clusters into a few genres.

Total box-office revenue tracked, by primary genre
Finding 06 · Return on investment

The highest-grossing genres aren't the best investments

Revenue alone rewards big-budget genres by default. Dividing profit by budget for each movie (limited to films with at least $10,000 in reported budget, to avoid inflated ratios from placeholder data) and taking the median within each genre tells a different story — some genres deliver strong typical returns on comparatively modest budgets. The median, not the average, is used here because a handful of ultra-low-budget breakout hits (a horror film made for $60,000 that grossed $248M, for instance) can pull a genre's mean ROI up to a number no typical movie in that genre comes close to. Genres with fewer than 20 financed titles are left off the chart below, since a median of a handful of movies is too noisy to compare fairly.

Finding 07 · Decades

Tracked box-office revenue has grown decade over decade

Summing revenue by the decade a movie was released (again limited to movies with usable TMDB financials) shows the scale of the modern box office next to earlier decades in this sample — part real industry growth, part a reflection of which older films happen to have complete TMDB financial records.

About this data

Sources

Ratings and movie titles/genres come from MovieLens 32M, a public research dataset from GroupLens Research at the University of Minnesota: 32,000,204 ratings across 87,585 movies from 200,948 users, collected between March 1996 and October 2023 (released May 2024) — the most recent MovieLens release GroupLens has published. Budget, revenue, runtime, popularity, and vote statistics come from The Movie Database (TMDB)'s public API, matched to each MovieLens movie via the tmdbId identifier in MovieLens's own links.csv crosswalk file, and reflect TMDB's numbers as of when this site was built (not adjusted for inflation). This product uses the TMDB API but is not endorsed or certified by TMDB.

Sampling this report is built on

The full 32M-rating release is too large to fetch financials for or load in a browser dashboard, so this report uses a reduced, reproducible subset: the 10,000 most-rated movies (out of 87,585) — which still account for 96.7% of all 32 million ratings — followed by a fixed-seed random sample of 400,000 ratings from the ~30.9 million ratings those movies received. The seed (42) and both thresholds are set in scripts/select_movies.py, so re-running the pipeline reproduces the same sample exactly. 157 of the 10,000 selected movies happened to draw zero ratings in this particular sample and are dropped, leaving 9,843 movies in this report.

What one row is

The underlying panel has one row per rating event: a single user rating a single movie at a specific time (userId, movieId, rating, and the year the rating was submitted). Each movie also carries static attributes joined onto every one of its ratings — genre, release year, decade, and (where available) budget, revenue, runtime, popularity, and vote statistics from TMDB.

Rows dropped or set aside

Beyond the sampling above, no additional rating rows were dropped. On the financial side: — of the 9,843 movies have no matching TMDB record or report $0 for both budget and revenue, which TMDB generally uses to mean "unknown," not "free" — these are excluded from every revenue, budget, profit, and ROI calculation (they remain in rating-based findings). Return on investment is left blank for any movie with a reported budget under $10,000, since a tiny or placeholder budget produces absurd ratios that would distort genre averages.

How every rate, ratio, and average is computed

Average rating is the mean of the 0.5–5.0 rating column for the relevant group. Primary genre is the first genre MovieLens lists for a movie; genre-level statistics group by this single primary genre, so genre buckets are mutually exclusive and don't double-count multi-genre movies. Profit is revenue minus budget. ROI is (revenue − budget) / budget, computed per movie; genre and headline ROI figures report the median ROI within each group rather than the mean, since ROI is a ratio that a handful of extreme low-budget hits can distort badly (see Finding 06). Decade is the movie's release year rounded down to the nearest 10. All financial figures are TMDB's reported (nominal) values, not adjusted for inflation.
